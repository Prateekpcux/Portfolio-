The Real Problem

It wasn't a productivity problem. It was a compliance crisis.

Inova had blocked external AI tools — ChatGPT, Copilot — across its network for good reason. PHI protection, HIPAA compliance, data governance. But blocking the tools didn't remove the need. Employees were using AI anyway, on personal devices, outside any oversight.

The existing internal tool, SHARP, was a basic chat wrapper. It couldn't create a ServiceNow ticket, couldn't analyze a document, and had no concept of what was appropriate to ask in a healthcare context. So the gap between "approved AI" and "useful AI" was being filled by employees making judgment calls on their personal phones with patient-adjacent data.

The Shadow AI Problem
  • ChatGPT and Copilot blocked at the network level — but accessible on personal devices
  • 74% of employees surveyed had used a non-approved AI tool for work in the past month
  • No visibility for IT or compliance teams into what data was being shared
  • SHARP couldn't handle the two most requested workflows: ticket creation and document analysis
The Design Mandate
  • Build an AI assistant employees would actually choose over their personal phones
  • PHI-safe by design — not just by policy. The interface itself needed to enforce boundaries
  • Governed from day one — Terms of Use gate, usage transparency, concern reporting
  • Fast enough to compete with consumer AI — not a downgrade
Discovery

What the workshops revealed.

I ran discovery workshops with clinical staff, IT, compliance, and operations leads. Three findings shaped every design decision that followed.

⚠️

The tool gap was real and being worked around

Employees weren't using personal AI out of recklessness — they were solving real workflow problems. ServiceNow ticket creation alone took an average of 11 minutes. They needed a faster path.

🔒

Trust required transparency, not just policy

Users needed to know exactly what the AI could and couldn't do before they started typing. A reminder at the bottom of the screen wasn't enough — they needed to actively acknowledge boundaries before accessing the tool.

⚡

Speed was non-negotiable

If the governed tool was slower or less capable than the personal phone alternative, adoption would fail. The LLM switcher wasn't a settings feature — it was the product's answer to "why use this instead of ChatGPT?"

Feature Scoping

What we built first — and why.

ServiceNow ticket creation was the most requested feature from discovery. It also had the most complex API dependency. I pushed for it as Phase 2 — not because it wasn't important, but because a broken ServiceNow integration on launch day would destroy trust in the entire product. We needed the chat experience to be flawless first.

P1Chat interface + conversation history
P1Document upload + analysis
P1LLM model switcher (Fast 4.1 / Thinking 5.0)
P1Terms of Use gate + compliance modal
P1Report a Concern — model feedback loop
Phase 2 — Post-launch
P2ServiceNow ticket creation + status
P2Microsoft 365 integration (Word, Excel)
P2Settings + preferences panel
P2Dark mode

Why ServiceNow is Phase 2: API integration timeline was outside our control. Shipping a broken workflow automation on launch day would have been worse than not having it — it would have destroyed trust in everything else.

User Flow

Two flows. One compliance gate.

New users hit the Terms of Use modal before accessing the dashboard — this was a deliberate compliance decision, not a legal afterthought. Returning users go straight to the dashboard. The compliance gate only runs once, but its content stays accessible at any time via the footer link.

Critical Design Decisions

Three decisions that made
this different from just a chatbot.

Any engineer could wrap an LLM in a text box. The design problem was making a tool that healthcare employees would trust enough to use instead of their personal phones. These three decisions are what got us there.

01

Examples / Capabilities / Limitations on the dashboard home

The first thing a user sees isn't a blank chat box — it's a 3-column breakdown of what the AI can do, remembers, and gets wrong. Borrowed from the original ChatGPT pattern but adapted specifically for a healthcare context. Clinical examples, not generic ones. This wasn't decorative — it was the trust layer that replaced a training session.

→ Users knew what to ask from session one
02

Terms of Use as a blocking modal, not a footer link

Compliance wanted a footer link. I pushed for a blocking modal on first use — four specific bullet points: no PHI, no sensitive data, AI may be wrong, HIPAA applies. Cancel or Accept. No middle ground. The argument: a footer link gets ignored. A blocking modal with explicit bullet points creates accountability. Legal agreed after the second conversation.

→ Zero ambiguity about permitted use
03

LLM switcher as a visible, always-accessible control

Fast (4.1) for quick queries. Thinking (5.0) for complex analysis. The switcher lives at the top of every screen — not in settings. The reason: if a user doesn't know they can switch models, they'll assume the AI is always slow or always fast, and calibrate expectations wrong. Transparency about the model being used is a trust feature, not a power-user feature.

→ Users matched model to task deliberately

Component System

Built from scratch.
Inter. Dark navy. Healthcare-first.

I chose Inter over system fonts — it renders more consistently across monitors, which matters in a clinical desktop environment. The dark navy sidebar (`#0D1B5E`) comes directly from Inova's brand system. Every component was built as a reusable Figma element — the sidebar, LLM dropdown, search bar, buttons, and context menus — so engineering had a clear spec and we had zero rework during handoff.

Designing AI for healthcare isn't about making the most powerful tool — it's about making the most trustworthy one. Every decision in Inova Assist was a trust decision first. The compliance gate, the limitations disclosure, the concern reporting, the model transparency — none of these were legal requirements. They were UX decisions that made a governed tool feel better than an ungoverned one. That's how you get staff off their personal phones.

What I'd do differently

Ship the ServiceNow integration in Phase 1.

In hindsight, the ServiceNow delay was the right call technically but wrong strategically. It was the single feature that would have driven the most immediate, measurable value for clinical staff. If I could redo the timeline, I'd have started the API discovery work in parallel with design rather than sequentially. The gap cost us two sprints we didn't have to lose.

What this proved

Governance is a product feature, not a legal layer.

The most important design insight from this project: when you're building AI for a regulated environment, the compliance decisions are the product decisions. The Terms of Use modal, the non-medical use reminder, the concern reporting — these aren't overhead. They're what makes the tool safe enough to use at work. Healthcare AI design is trust design. Everything else is execution.
