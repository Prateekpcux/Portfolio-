What I Learned

Three findings shaped every decision.

I ran workshops with clinical staff, IT, compliance, and operations. Three things stood out.

📋

The old tool gave up on long files

Clinical notes and policies are long. The hospital's old assistant couldn't read them. The fix was simple: use a model that handles long text.

🔒

Safety had to be felt, not buried

A small note in the footer gets ignored. People needed to see the rules and agree to them before they start typing.

⚡

Speed decides if people switch

If it felt slower than ChatGPT, no one would use it. So I gave them a fast model and a slower, deeper-thinking one.

The Decisions That Mattered

Three choices that made this
more than a chatbot in a box.

Putting a text box on top of ChatGPT takes an afternoon. Getting healthcare staff to trust it enough to switch is the real work. These three did it.

01

Show what the AI can and can't do - on the first screen

A new chat opens with three plain columns: what it's good at, what it remembers, and where it gets things wrong. No training needed. People knew what to ask from minute one.

→ People knew what to expect
02

Make people agree to the rules before they start

Compliance wanted a footer link. I pushed for a one-time screen with four plain points: no patient data, no sensitive data, the AI can be wrong, hospital rules still apply. Read it, then continue. Legal said yes after one look.

→ No confusion about what's allowed
03

Let people pick the AI's speed - out in the open

Fast for quick answers. Thinking for hard problems. I kept the switch at the top of every screen, not hidden in settings, so people match it to the job instead of guessing.

→ Right tool for the task

A Closer Look

Summing up a meeting,
in three steps.

Staff sit through long meetings and just need the decisions and to-dos. I gave this its own button in the sidebar and kept the whole thing to three steps. The summary lands right in the chat - so you can ask follow-up questions about it, not just read it.

1

Pick a date

Choose the day the meeting happened.

2

Pick a meeting

Choose from that day's list.

3

Get the summary

It appears in the chat, ready to question.

Step 1 · Pick a date

Choose the day

One choice per screen. The calendar opens on a set date, so no one starts from a blank box. One button forward: Next.

Step 2 · Pick a meeting

Choose from that day

The date stays put, still changeable. Each meeting shows how long it ran, so a quick sync reads differently from a long one. Pick one, then Create summary.

Step 3 · Read and ask

The summary lands right in the chat

Subject, who was there, a short overview, then the full notes - as a message you can reply to, not a file you have to open. The text box stays open, so the next question is one line away.

Why this choice matters

Most summary tools dump a file and stop. By dropping the summary back into the chat, people can keep going - pull the to-dos, double-check a decision, draft the follow-up email - without leaving. That one choice is why a meeting summarizer belongs inside the assistant, not off in a separate report.

Results From Testing

Two numbers.
Both from real users.

I tested with two groups of 8 - clinical staff, operations, and IT. These two numbers are the ones worth talking about.

91%
finished their task - up from 68% in round one
What I changed between rounds
  • →People got stuck on the agreement screen. "Cancel" looked like "quit." I changed the buttons to "Accept" and "Decline." The drop-off went to zero.
  • →People ignored the model numbers. Adding the words "Fast" and "Thinking" fixed it right away.
  • →Upload was hard to find. Moving the icon solved it in one round.
8/8
said they'd use it instead of their old workarounds
How I asked
  • →I asked: would you use this, or find another way? In round two, all 8 said yes - up from 5.
  • →The 3 who said no the first time all named the same two issues. Both fixed before round two.
  • →This is the number that counts. The tool only wins if it replaces the workarounds - not just if people can finish a task in a test.

Designing AI for healthcare isn't about the most powerful tool. It's about the one people trust. Every choice here - the agreement, the reminders, the report button, showing which model you're on - was a trust choice first. None were legally required. They're the reason people use a safe tool instead of a risky one.

What I'd do differently

Start the ticket feature sooner.

Holding it for later was the right call, but it cost us. Auto-creating IT tickets was the thing staff missed most when ChatGPT was blocked. If I could redo it, I'd start that work alongside the design instead of after it. We lost two weeks we didn't need to.

The bigger lesson

In healthcare, safety is the product.

The agreement, the reminders, the report button - these aren't extras. They're what makes the tool usable at work. Without them you don't have a healthcare AI product. You have a chatbot that happens to run inside a hospital. That difference is the whole job.
